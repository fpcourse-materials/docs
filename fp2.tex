    \section*{Introduction}

    Many complex concepts in language design and programming can be understood as special cases of some simple fundamental principles that are usually treated as common folklore and do not require extra explanation.
    However, the difficulty is that this knowledge is scattered across books, papers, and “cult” blog posts, and it takes a lot of time and effort to reconstruct the whole picture.

    The goal of this course is to gather such folklore knowledge in one place and organize it into a coherent system.
    The course will explicitly rely on classical works studying the principles of language design and will help in studying them.
    Reading the referenced papers is an important part of independent work in the course.

    By functional programming, as highlighted in the course title, we mean a careful attitude towards the notion of effect, which in FP — unlike in other schools of thought — is not considered an axiomatic given but a subject for study, conscious construction, and careful handling.
    This approach turns out to be very useful for learning languages and for building powerful language constructs; it is also the foundation of a productive programming style.
    Moreover, functional languages are relatively simple, which means new ideas and approaches often originate there and spread further.

    Haskell is chosen as the main language of the course because, on the one hand, it embodies many concepts that are often brought to a certain logical completion and is powerful enough to encode others.
    On the other hand, it remains a practical industrial programming language.

    Because of the breadth of the topic, this course is not always deep.
    Thus, implementation details in GHC or the category-theoretic foundations of some concepts may be given in general terms, without going into specifics.
    At the same time, in the realm of language design through the lens of functional programming, the course tries to be as detailed as possible.

    Thus, this course may be useful for those who are interested in language design and elegant generalizations of programming concepts, want to improve their API design skills, or plan to work practically with functional languages.

    A prerequisite for the course is knowledge of the basics of functional programming: algebraic data types, pattern matching, folds, parametric polymorphism, type classes, and basic monads.
    It is also useful to be able to read type judgments, and to be familiar with polymorphic lambda calculus and Church encoding.
